# domains/procedural.md — skills that need practice (`teach/v1`)

Use for: languages, instruments, tools and software workflows, lab and clinical procedures,
craft skills, exam technique, anything where knowing the steps and being able to do them are
different states.

The defining property: **explanation is nearly worthless here**. The learner can recite the
procedure perfectly and fail to execute it. So this pack shifts almost everything from
explaining to doing, and the checkpoint is a performance, not a question about a performance.

## The unit is a rep, not a step

- A node is one **executable sub-skill** — small enough to attempt in the session, whole enough
  to succeed or fail visibly.
- A teaching step is: demonstrate once -> they attempt -> immediate specific feedback -> they
  attempt again. Two attempts minimum before moving on. Immediate feedback is the whole
  mechanism.
- Feedback names the **action**, never the person, and names exactly one thing to change per
  rep: "hold the vowel a beat longer" beats "the pronunciation is off."

## What a checkpoint looks like here

A performance, captured in text because that is the channel Stage 0 has:

- **Language:** produce the sentence, in the target tense, about their own life. Not "which is
  correct".
- **Tools / software:** write the exact command or the exact sequence, for their real situation.
- **Procedure:** list the steps *in order*, from memory, including the check step and the
  failure branch.
- **Exam technique:** do the question under the real constraint (time, no notes) and report what
  they did, in order.

Then a graded MC item alongside it, so the event has a recordable `correct` value and a named
distractor set. The MC records the *decision points* of the procedure: what to do when the
expected thing does not happen.

## Distractors from real failure modes

Procedural distractors are the classic errors of execution, not of understanding:

- Correct steps in the wrong order.
- A step omitted that only matters sometimes (the check, the flush, the backup, the confirm).
- The right action applied to the wrong object or at the wrong time.
- The recovery step for a *different* failure.
- A shortcut that works in the demo case and breaks in the general one.

## Spacing and interleaving matter more here than anywhere

- Two short sessions beat one long one; massed practice produces performance that does not
  survive the week.
- **Interleave** related sub-skills rather than blocking them: mixing makes practice feel worse
  and retention better — a desirable difficulty (Bjork) *(verify)*.
- Every session opens with one delayed retrieval rep on a previous node, before any new node.
  Record it `--context delayed`.

## Verbalize sparingly

Ask the learner to state the rule *after* they can do it, not before. Verbalizing a procedure
before it is fluent can interfere with executing it. Teach-back in this domain is therefore
scheduled later in a node's life than in the other packs — after two independent passes, not
after the first.

When you do run teach-back here, ask for the **decision rule**, not the step list:
*"how do you know which one to use?"*

## What you cannot observe, you do not record

Stage 0 is text. If the skill has a physical or real-time component you cannot see — pronunciation,
technique, timing under pressure — say so, and record only what you actually observed. Do not
record a pass on something you could not check. An honest gap in the map is worth more than a
fabricated pass.

## Misconception hot spots

- Knowing the steps mistaken for being able to do them (the fluency illusion, in its purest
  form).
- A memorized happy path with no failure branch.
- Cargo-culted steps whose purpose is unknown — always ask "what does that step prevent?".
- Transferring a rule from a similar tool or language where it does not apply.
